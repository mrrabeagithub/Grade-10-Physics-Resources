Reflection of Light

Like any wave, when light encounters a surface or obstacle it cannot pass through, it bounces back. Discover the laws governing this fundamental optical phenomenon.

1. Types of Reflection

This section introduces the foundational concepts of surface interaction. By exploring how light behaves on different textures, we understand why mirrors form clear images while ordinary objects simply become visible. Interact with the buttons below to compare Regular and Diffuse reflection.

Select Surface Type

Regular Reflection

When parallel light rays strike a smooth surface, all of the rays are reflected at the same angle in the same forward direction. This forms a clear image.

Example: Plane mirrors, calm water.

!

The Reversibility of Light: The path followed by light is independent of its direction of travel. If you reverse the direction of a ray, it will follow exactly the same path backward.

2. The Laws of Reflection

Here we visualize the strict geometric rules that govern specular reflection. Use the interactive simulator below to change the angle of the incoming light ray and observe how the reflected ray responds according to the Second Law of Reflection.

The Two Laws

  • 1. The incident ray, the reflected ray, and the normal at the point of incidence all lie in the same plane.
  • 2. The angle of incidence is equal to the angle of reflection (î = r̂).

4. Field of Vision

The field of view is the space an observer can perceive when looking in a mirror. This section compares how different mirror geometries—specifically flat vs. convex—impact visibility, visualizing why certain mirrors are chosen for vehicle safety and security.

How to Increase Field of Vision

  • Increase the size of the mirror: Larger mirror = wider angles of incidence/reflection captured.
  • Bring the observer closer: Decreasing distance expands the angular sweep of the reflected rays entering the eye.
  • Use a Convex Mirror: A spherical mirror curved outward diverges light rays, dramatically expanding the perceivable area.
Real-World Application: Because their field of vision is larger, convex mirrors are heavily used as rear-view and side mirrors in cars, motorcycles, and at blind crossroads.
