The Laws of Reflection

This section explores the fundamental geometry of light bouncing off a boundary. The interactive simulator below proves the core principle: the angle at which light strikes a surface is exactly equal to the angle at which it reflects.

Key Definitions

  • Incident Ray: The incoming light beam.
  • Reflected Ray: The outgoing light beam.
  • Normal: An imaginary line drawn exactly perpendicular (90°) to the mirror surface at the point of incidence.

Specular vs. Diffuse Reflection

The texture of a surface completely changes how light behaves. This section compares reflection from perfectly smooth surfaces (like mirrors) to rough surfaces (like paper or asphalt).

Regular (Specular) Reflection

Occurs on smooth surfaces (mirrors, still water).

  • ✔ Parallel incoming rays stay parallel after reflecting.
  • ✔ Reflects light in one specific forward direction.
  • ✔ Forms a clear, sharp image.

Diffuse Reflection

Occurs on rough surfaces (paper, walls, dry roads).

  • ✔ Parallel rays are scattered in many different directions.
  • ✔ No clear image is formed.
  • ✔ Allows us to see the object itself from any angle.

🌧 Real-World Physics: The "Puddle Effect"

Have you noticed it's harder to see the road while driving at night when it's raining?

Dry Road: The asphalt is rough. Headlights undergo Diffuse Reflection, scattering light in all directions, including back to the driver's eyes, making the road visible.
Wet Road: Water fills the rough gaps, creating a smooth surface. Headlights undergo Regular (Specular) Reflection, bouncing the light far forward like a mirror. Little light returns to the driver, and it causes severe glare for oncoming cars!

Images in a Plane Mirror

A plane (flat) mirror forms an image of any object placed in front of it. This section details the five critical characteristics of these images and visually maps the relationship between object and image geometry.

👻

Virtual

Forms behind mirror; cannot be projected.

↑

Upright

Appears the same way up as the object.

📏

Same Size

No magnification occurs.

↔

Equidistant

Object distance (p) = Image distance (q).

👋

Laterally Inverted

Left becomes right, and vice versa.

Conceptual Flashcards

🔍

Why does a mirror reverse left to right, but not up and down?

(Click to reveal answer)

Answer

Mirrors don't actually reverse left and right; they reverse front and back (depth). Your nose is closer to the mirror than your ears, so the image's nose is closer to the glass. This depth inversion creates the illusion of lateral inversion.

💫

What creates multiple images between two flat mirrors?

(Click to reveal answer)

Answer

When two mirrors face each other exactly parallel, the reflected light bounces back and forth infinitely. The image in the first mirror acts as the "object" for the second mirror, and so on, creating an endless tunnel effect.

🏢

Why are some shop windows slanted inward at the bottom?

(Click to reveal answer)

Answer

Slanting the window changes the normal line. It reflects the glare of bright sky or buildings downwards (toward the sidewalk) rather than straight back into the eyes of shoppers, allowing them to see the display inside clearly.

📐

If a wall is a mirror, how much larger does the room appear?

(Click to reveal answer)

Answer

It appears twice as large. Because the image distance equals the object distance ($p=q$), the reflection creates a virtual extension of the room behind the mirror that is exactly the same depth as the actual room.
